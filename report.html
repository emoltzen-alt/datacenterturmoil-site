National Trends

Local opposition to data center construction remains one of the fastest-moving land-use stories in the country heading into the 2026 midterms. Recent national polling (Heatmap Pro, Aug. 8–13, 2026) found roughly three-quarters of registered voters oppose new data center construction near their homes, up sharply from a year earlier. Data Center Watch estimates opponents have blocked or delayed more than $130 billion in projects nationwide in 2026 alone, and the number of local moratoriums enacted this year is already about four times the total for all of 2025.

  • Recurring flashpoints: water consumption for cooling, strain on the electric grid and ratepayer costs, 24/7 industrial noise, loss of rural/agricultural land, and lack of transparency in developer negotiations.
  • Recurring venues: county planning/zoning commissions, city councils, and county boards of commissioners/supervisors considering moratoriums, rezonings, and conditional-use permits.
  • Escalation pattern: disputes are increasingly moving from public hearings into litigation — both residents suing local governments and developers, and in at least one case a developer suing residents.

St. Louis, Missouri Current
Board of Aldermen passes citywide data center zoning rules, sending them to the mayor

After roughly a year of debate that began when a developer's plan for a data center inside the historic Midtown Armory came to light, the St. Louis Board of Aldermen unanimously passed Board Bill 49 on Friday. The ordinance classifies data centers by size, restricts where they can be built (largely to industrial zones), and imposes renewable-energy, lighting, and noise requirements. It now goes to Mayor Cara Spencer for signature. The bill is a direct response to sustained resident pushback, including an August lawsuit by neighbors and advocacy groups over the Armory proposal.
